Source: Kellen Funk and Lincoln A. Mullen, “The Spine of American Law: Digital Text Analysis and U.S. Legal Practice,” American Historical Review 123, no. 1 (2018): 132–164, doi:10.1093/ahr/123.1.132, figure 5, and the article's research compendium (the best matches between sections, as of February 2018).

Notes: Each node is a code or statute of civil procedure from the article's corpus of 222, labelled by its jurisdiction and the year of its enactment; an arrow runs from the code borrowed from to the code that borrowed, and is wider the more sections it carried. A borrowing counts the sections of the later code whose text most resembles a section of the earlier one: the article split every code into sections, compared them by the five-word phrases they share (minhashing and locality-sensitive hashing, then the Jaccard similarity of the candidates), and matched each section to at most one earlier section, preferring a code of the same state and the nearest year. The network keeps a borrowing of at least 50 sections or a fifth of the borrowing code's sections, and at most the two largest for each code, dropping the second when it comes from the same state as the first and not from the borrower's own; the 186 codes with a borrowing left are shown, the other 36 are not. A code is coloured by its distance from one of the seven drafts of New York's Field Code (1848, 1849, 1850, 1851, 1853, 1864 and 1873) along the arrows: an original borrower took text from a draft directly, a subsequent borrower through one or more other codes, and a code that did not borrow owes nothing to one, though it may have borrowed from another code, as Michigan's 1847 statute did from its 1839 one. The three codes of Dakota Territory are labelled, as in the article, by the state that later took them (ND1862, ND1868, SD1887). The layout is a force simulation, not the figure's hand-finished arrangement: a node's place carries no meaning beyond its connections, and the layout is the same on every visit. Drag a node to move it, scroll to zoom and drag the background to pan. Choosing a year shows the codes enacted by then, in place; choosing a code traces its line of descent, the codes it drew on and the codes that drew on it, as far as the year allows, and hovering a code names its sources and borrowers. The count of sections is of every chunk the automatic splitting produced, headings and tables of contents among them, so a code's total somewhat overstates its sections, and a short statute split badly has very few.
