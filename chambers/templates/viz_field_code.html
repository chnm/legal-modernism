Source: Kellen Funk and Lincoln A. Mullen, “The Spine of American Law: Digital Text Analysis and U.S. Legal Practice,” American Historical Review 123, no. 1 (2018): 132–164, doi:10.1093/ahr/123.1.132, figure 5, and the article's research compendium.

Notes: Each node is a code or statute of civil procedure from the article's corpus, labelled by its jurisdiction and the year of its enactment; an arrow runs from the code borrowed from to the code that borrowed, and is wider the more sections it carried. A borrowing counts the sections of the later code whose text most resembles a section of the earlier one: the article split every code into sections, compared them by the five-word phrases they share (minhashing and locality-sensitive hashing, then the Jaccard similarity of the candidates), and matched each section to at most one earlier section, preferring a code of the same state and the nearest year. The figure keeps a borrowing of at least 50 sections or a fifth of the borrowing code's sections, and at most the two largest for each code, dropping the second when it comes from the same state as the first and not from the borrower's own; the 186 codes with a borrowing left are shown, the other 36 of the corpus are not. The nodes and their labels stand where the published figure placed them, which its authors settled by hand after a force-directed layout, stretched by a twelfth to give the labels room, with some forty codes moved a few pixels, or their labels set on the other side of the dot, where labels overlapped on screen. A code is colored by its distance from one of the seven drafts of New York's Field Code (1848, 1849, 1850, 1851, 1853, 1864 and 1873) along the arrows: an original borrower took text from a draft directly, a subsequent borrower through one or more other codes, and a code that did not borrow owes nothing to one, though it may have borrowed from another code, as Michigan's 1847 statute did from its 1839 one. The three codes of Dakota Territory are labelled, as in the article, by the state that later took them (ND1862, ND1868, SD1887). The colors can instead show each code's region, the regions the project's other charts use, drawn from the Census Bureau's regions and divisions, with New York set apart in the Field codes' red: New York, New England, the Midwest, the South, the Mountain West and the Pacific West, and the United Kingdom for its one statute (UK1852, which the article's corpus calls GB1852). Drag a node to move it, drag the background to pan, and zoom with the + and − buttons (or by pinching on a touch screen; the mouse wheel scrolls the page); Reset view restores the frame. Choosing a code traces its line of descent, the codes it drew on and the codes that drew on it. Hovering a code lists the codes it borrowed from and the codes that borrowed from it, every borrowing of ten sections or more, with the sections and their share of the borrowing code's sections; the rows in grey are the borrowings the figure does not draw, too small for it or with a code it leaves out, and clicking the code pins the list so a long one can be scrolled. The count of sections is of every chunk the automatic splitting produced, headings and tables of contents among them, so a code's total somewhat overstates its sections, and a short statute split badly has very few.
